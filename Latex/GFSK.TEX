\begin{abstract}
Gaussian Frequency Shift Keying (GFSK) offers constant-envelope, bandwidth-efficient transmission and is widely adopted in low-power links. Burst reception in low SNR conditions is challenging due to unknown start time, carrier-frequency offset (CFO) and timing error. This paper presents a noncoherent burst GFSK demodulator implemented on FPGA. We design a differential-correlation frame synchronizer with adaptive threshold, a data-aided CFO estimator (Luise--Reggiannini), a phase-peak timing recovery, and a multi-sample differential detector that improves bit-error performance by 1--2 dB. MATLAB floating- and fixed-point simulations validate the algorithms; a Verilog implementation on Xilinx FPGA (10 MHz clock) achieves 500 kHz symbol rate. Hardware co-simulation demonstrates BER of $2.99\times10^{-4}$ at $E_b/N_0=14$ dB and $9.3\times10^{-6}$ at $17$ dB.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Gaussian frequency shift keying, burst reception, noncoherent demodulation, synchronization, FPGA.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
GFSK is attractive for IoT, telemetry, and space links because of its constant envelope and narrow spectrum. Burst reception under low SNR requires robust blind frame detection, CFO mitigation, timing recovery, and low-complexity demodulation suitable for FPGA.

This work proposes and implements a burst GFSK receiver that: 1) uses differential correlation and adaptive power threshold for frame detection; 2) performs data-aided CFO estimation with Luise--Reggiannini (L\&R) and derotation; 3) aligns timing via preamble phase peaks; 4) applies multi-sample differential detection to reduce BER; 5) realizes a complete RTL pipeline at 10 MHz achieving 500 kHz symbol rate with measured BER targets. The remainder is organized as follows: Section II gives the signal model; Section III revisits noncoherent differential demodulation; Section IV details burst receiver algorithms and pseudocode; Section V presents theoretical analysis; Sections VI--VIII report simulations, FPGA synthesis, and RTL co-simulation; Section IX concludes.

\section{GFSK Signal Model and Frame Format}
The continuous-time CPFSK baseband signal is
\begin{equation}
s(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2E_b}{T_b}}\exp\left\{j2\pi \frac{hR_b}{2}\int_{0}^{t} m(\tau)\,d\tau\right\},
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the modulation index, $R_b$ the symbol rate, $m(t)$ the Gaussian-filtered bipolar data. Sampling with $T_s=1/f_s$ yields
\begin{equation}
s[n]=\sqrt{\frac{2E_b}{T_b}}\exp\left\{j2\pi\frac{hR_b}{2f_s}\sum_{k=0}^{n} m[k]\right\}.
\end{equation}
A Gaussian filter with $BT=0.5$ and effective length $L=3$ shapes $m[n]$. We choose $f_s=20R_b$ for timing robustness.

Burst frame format: 4-byte preamble (repeating ``10''), 4-byte sync word (0x930B51DE), 1-byte length, payload (0--255 B), CRC16 (poly $x^{16}+x^{12}+x^{5}+1$).

\section{Noncoherent Differential Demodulation}
Single-sample differential detection decides on the sign of
\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta[n]=I[n]Q[n-N_T]-Q[n]I[n-N_T],
\end{equation}
where $N_T=f_s/R_b$ samples per symbol. Timing error reduces the effective phase swing and raises BER; noise smoothing also shrinks $\Delta\theta$. We therefore adopt multi-sample combining (Section~IV-E and Eq.~(9)) to improve reliability.

\section{Burst Receiver Algorithms}
\subsection{Low-Pass Front-End}
A linear-phase FIR low-pass filter (order $\approx 40$) suppresses out-of-band noise while keeping in-band distortion negligible.

\subsection{Frame Synchronization}
Using 4$\times$ oversampled data, we form a differential sequence $d[n]=z[n]z^{*}[n-N_x]$ ($N_x$ samples/symbol at 4$\times$ rate) to cancel CFO phase ramp, then correlate with the pre-differenced sync template. An adaptive threshold from instantaneous power avoids fixed-threshold failures. The peak of correlation marks the sync start and indirectly the preamble start.

\subsection{CFO Estimation and Compensation}
With the preamble, we form the matched sequence
\begin{equation}
c[n]=r[n]\,s_{\text{pre}}^{*}[n],
\end{equation}
and estimate the carrier-frequency offset (CFO) from $c[n]$ using one of several estimators. In this work we consider three data-aided estimators commonly used for burst signals:

(1) Fitz estimator. Fitz estimates CFO from a set of autocorrelations of $c[n]$:
\begin{equation}
R(m)=\frac{1}{L_0-m}\sum_{k=m}^{L_0}c[k]c^{*}[k-m],
\end{equation}
and forms an angle average (see \cite{Mengali2013}). Fitz is simple and has low complexity, but its unambiguous range is limited and it requires phase unwrapping when the offset is large.

(2) Kay estimator. Kay's estimator uses first-difference phase measurements and a weighted sum to approach maximum-likelihood performance at high SNR:
\begin{equation}
\hat{v}_{\text{Kay}}=\frac{1}{2\pi T_s}\sum_{k=1}^{L_0-1}\gamma(k)\Arg\{c[k]c^*[k-1]\},
\end{equation}
with weights $\gamma(k)$ chosen as in \cite{Mengali2013}. Kay performs well at moderate-to-high SNR but requires more per-sample phase operations.

(3) Luise--Reggiannini (L\&R). The L\&R estimator averages a small-range autocorrelation and extracts a single phase:
\begin{equation}
\hat{v}_{\text{L\&R}}=\frac{1}{\pi (N+1)T_s}\Arg\Big\{\sum_{m=1}^N R(m)\Big\},
\end{equation}
where $R(m)$ is defined above. L\&R attains a good trade-off between range, variance and hardware complexity; for this reason the RTL implementation uses L\&R together with a CORDIC for angle extraction.

After an estimator produces $\hat v$, the received samples are derotated as
\begin{equation}
r^{c}[n]=r[n]e^{-j2\pi \hat{v} n T_s},
\end{equation}
and subsequent timing recovery and demodulation operate on $r^{c}[n]$.

\subsection{Timing Recovery}
From derotated preamble, we locate phase peaks of consecutive ``10'' patterns, average their offsets to obtain timing shift $\bar{\epsilon}$, and adjust decision instants $n_0+\bar{\epsilon}+iN_T$.

\subsection{Multi-Sample Differential Detection}
We average $N_d$ phase-difference pairs around the nominal decision:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda[n]=\sum_{i=0}^{N_d-1}\left(I[n-i]Q[n-i+N_T]-Q[n-i]I[n-i+N_T]\right).
\end{equation}
Decide bit 1 if $\Lambda[n]>0$, else 0. $N_d$ trades BER gain for complexity. As shown in Algorithm~1, this approach is applied after timing recovery.

\subsection{Algorithmic Pseudocode (Simulation Pipeline)}
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Burst GFSK Receiver Simulation Pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} frame params (preamble, sync, len, CRC), $h$, $BT$, $f_s$, SNR, CFO $v$, timing offset, $N_T$, $N_d$
\State \textbf{Output:} BER, frame sync error $e_{\text{frame}}$, CFO error var$_f$, timing error var$_{\text{sync}}$
\State \textbf{Preprocessing:} generate random payload; append CRC16; map to bipolar symbols; Gaussian filter and upsample by $f_s$; add random delay for each transmission; form $s[n]$
\State \textbf{Channel:} apply CFO $e^{j2\pi v nT_s}$ and AWGN to get $r[n]$
\State \textbf{LPF:} filter $r[n]$ with FIR $\rightarrow z[n]$
\State \textbf{Frame sync:} build $d[n]=z[n]z^{*}[n-N_x]$; correlate with diff-sync; adaptive threshold; find peak $\rightarrow$ sync index
\State \textbf{CFO:} extract preamble segment; compute $c[n]=r[n]s_{\text{pre}}^{*}[n]$; apply L\&R to get $\hat{v}$; derotate $r_c[n]=r[n]e^{-j2\pi \hat{v} nT_s}$
\State \textbf{Timing:} find phase peaks over multiple ``10'' periods in $r_c[n]$; average to get $\bar{\epsilon}$; set decision instants
\State \textbf{Demod:} for each symbol, compute $\Lambda[n]$ with $N_d$ pairs; hard-decision bits; strip preamble/sync/len; CRC check
\State \textbf{Metrics:} compare to ground truth; accumulate BER, var$_f$, var$_{\text{sync}}$, $e_{\text{frame}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\subsection{Implementation notes, complexity and practical parameters}
This subsection summarizes practical implementation details and resource/complexity considerations for the proposed burst receiver.

Computational complexity and latency: the dominant cost in frame synchronization is the differential correlation (approximately $L_{\text{sync}}\times N_x$ complex multiplies per test position, where $L_{\text{sync}}$ is the sync length and $N_x$ the samples/symbol used for sync). CFO estimation (L\&R) requires $O(N\cdot L_0)$ complex products and a single angle extraction (CORDIC). The multi-sample differential demodulator incurs roughly $N_d$ complex multiplies per decision (or equivalent real operations after optimization). These estimates guide partitioning between parallel DSPs and serialized datapaths.

Memory and LUTs: implement circular buffers sized for $f_s$ sampling (we use 256 samples for store/rollback in this work) and smaller $f_x$ buffers for differential correlation. Store precomputed templates (preamble, diff-sync) and sine/CORDIC tables in BRAM/ROM to reduce run-time computation.

Hardware resources: the design relies on a CORDIC block (for phase extraction/rotation), several DSP slices for complex multiplications, and BRAM for buffers and lookup tables. When targeting low-end FPGAs, trade off $N_d$ and sync oversampling (4$\times$ used here) to fit available DSP/BRAM resources.

Recommended parameters and robustness tips: use $f_s=20R_b$ (so $N_T=20$ samples/symbol) for timing robustness; 4$\times$ oversampling for frame sync to reduce correlation cost; an example working choice is $N_d=7$ for moderate SNRs (as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ber_multisample}). Employ the adaptive power-based threshold described in Sec.~IV-B to avoid fixed-threshold failures. Quantize internal signals with sufficient fractional bits (we used 10 fractional bits in fixed-point simulations) and validate with MATLAB fixed-point simulation before RTL.

Validation: verify functionality and performance via MATLAB-generated stimuli feeding the Vivado testbench (as in Sec.~V). Fit an empirical $\Delta\phi_{\rm eff}$ from simulation/RTL BER curves to align theory and measurements and to guide parameter tuning.

\section{Performance Analysis}
\subsection{CFO Estimation Error}
For L\&R, the variance approximates \cite{Bian2003}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{var}(\hat{v}) \approx \frac{6}{(N+1)(N-1)}\frac{1}{(2\pi T_s)^2}\frac{1}{\mathrm{SNR}},
\end{equation}
assuming small $v$ and high SNR; it approaches the CRB as $N$ grows.

\subsection{Timing Error Impact}
A timing error $\epsilon T_s$ reduces effective phase swing by factor $g(\epsilon)\approx\cos(\pi\epsilon/N_T)$ for smoothed CPFSK, yielding an SNR loss $G_{\epsilon}=g^2(\epsilon)$ in the differential detector. As demonstrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ber_sync_ablation}, proper timing recovery and CFO compensation significantly improve BER performance.

\subsubsection{Signal model and assumptions}
We consider the receiver after coarse synchronization and CFO compensation. At the correct sampling (decision) instant the complex baseband samples are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rx_samples}
r_k = s_k + n_k,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},
\end{equation}
where $s_k$ is the noise-free GFSK/CPFSK complex envelope and $n_k$ is circular complex AWGN
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise}
n_k\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,N_0),\quad \mathbb{E}\{|n_k|^2\}=N_0.
\end{equation}
For constant-envelope CPM signals we write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sk}
s_k=\sqrt{E_b}\,e^{j\theta_k},
\end{equation}
and define the phase increment
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_theta}
\Delta\theta_k\triangleq\theta_k-\theta_{k-1}.
\end{equation}
For binary CPM/GFSK at ideal decision instants the phase increment can be approximated by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:delta_phi}
\Delta\theta_k\approx
\begin{cases}
+\Delta\phi,& b_k=1,\\
-\Delta\phi,& b_k=0,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\phi$ is the effective phase opening (for rectangular pulses $\Delta\phi\approx\pi h$; for GFSK we use an empirical $\Delta\phi_{\rm eff}$ to absorb ISI and timing effects).

\subsubsection{Decision statistic (differential detector)}
The receiver forms the differential product
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:z_def}
z_k\triangleq r_k r_{k-1}^*.
\end{equation}
In the absence of noise
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sk_product}
s_k s_{k-1}^*=E_b e^{j\Delta\theta_k},
\end{equation}
so that the imaginary part carries the decision information:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:im_ss}
\Im\{s_k s_{k-1}^*\}=E_b\sin(\Delta\theta_k)\approx\pm E_b\sin(\Delta\phi).
\end{equation}
We therefore use the statistic
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:y_stat}
y_k\triangleq\Im\{r_k r_{k-1}^*\},
\end{equation}
and decide $\hat b_k=1$ if $y_k>0$, else $\hat b_k=0$.

\subsubsection{Noise expansion and moments}
Substitute $r_k=s_k+n_k$ into $z_k$:
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-10}
z_k=(s_k+n_k)(s_{k-1}^*+n_{k-1}^*)
=s_k s_{k-1}^*+s_k n_{k-1}^*+n_k s_{k-1}^*+n_k n_{k-1}^*.
\end{equation}
Taking imaginary parts gives
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-11}
y_k=\Im\{s_k s_{k-1}^*\}+\Im\{s_k n_{k-1}^*\}+\Im\{n_k s_{k-1}^*\}+\Im\{n_k n_{k-1}^*\}.
\end{equation}
Because the noise has zero mean and is independent of the signal, the conditional mean under $b_k$ is
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-13}
\mu_{y|b}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\{y_k\mid b_k\}=\Im\{s_k s_{k-1}^*\}=\pm E_b\sin(\Delta\phi).
\end{equation}
Define the noise part $\eta_k$ as the sum of the last three terms in (X-11). Its variance is
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-15}
\sigma_y^2=\mathbb{E}\{\eta_k^2\}.
\end{equation}
By standard circular-Gaussian properties,
\begin{align}
\tag{X-18}
\mathrm{Var}\big(\Im\{s_k n_{k-1}^*\}\big)&=\frac{E_bN_0}{2},\\
\tag{X-19}
\mathrm{Var}\big(\Im\{n_k s_{k-1}^*\}\big)&=\frac{E_bN_0}{2},
\end{align}
which sum to $E_bN_0$. The term $\Im\{n_k n_{k-1}^*\}$ contributes approximately $N_0^2/2$ (engineering approximation), hence
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-23}
\sigma_y^2\approx E_bN_0+\frac{N_0^2}{2}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{BER approximation}
Approximating $\eta_k$ as Gaussian, the conditional distribution under $b_k=1$ is
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-25}
y_k\mid b_k=1\sim\mathcal{N}\big(+E_b\sin(\Delta\phi),\;\sigma_y^2\big).
\end{equation}
Thus the bit error probability is approximated by
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-26}
P_b\approx Q\!\left(\frac{E_b\sin(\Delta\phi)}{\sqrt{E_bN_0+\frac{N_0^2}{2}}}\right).
\end{equation}
With $\gamma\triangleq E_b/N_0$ this simplifies to the practical closed-form approximation
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-28}
P_b\approx Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma\sin(\Delta\phi)}{\sqrt{\gamma+\tfrac12}}\right).
\end{equation}
This form is convenient for engineering use by replacing $\Delta\phi$ with an empirical $\Delta\phi_{\rm eff}$ that captures ISI, timing error and multi-sample averaging effects.

% Chernoff-style upper bound (exponential form) and engineering fitting
An exponential upper-bound (Chernoff) commonly used in scripts is
\begin{equation}
\tag{X-33}
P_e\lesssim\frac12\exp\big\{-\gamma\sin^2(\tfrac{\Delta\phi}{2})\big\},
\end{equation}
which explains the exponential models used for curve fitting; it should be presented as an approximation/upper bound in the paper.

% Practical recommendation
In practice one fits $\Delta\phi_{\rm eff}$ (for example at $E_b/N_0=13\,$dB) to align the analytical curve with MATLAB/RTL results; for our implementation ($h=0.7$, $BT=0.5$) the fitted effective opening was found to be approximately $1.424\,$rad.

\section{FPGA Architecture and Synthesis}
The burst GFSK receiver is implemented on a Xilinx Artix-7 FPGA (xc7a35t). The hardware architecture, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:gfsk_arch}, consists of a clock generator, a shared circular buffer, and specialized processing cores for frame synchronization, frequency offset estimation, timing recovery, and demodulation. A finite state machine (FSM) in the control logic coordinates the sequential activation of these modules to process each burst packet.

\subsection{Clock Generation and Data Storage}
A single 10 MHz master clock drives the system. A clock divider module generates two derived enable signals: $clk_1$ (0.5 MHz) for symbol-rate operations and $clk_4$ (2 MHz) for 4$\times$ oversampling during frame synchronization.
Input I/Q samples are stored in a dual-port RAM configured as a circular buffer (depth 256). This shared memory architecture minimizes resource usage by allowing the sync detector, frequency estimator, and demodulator to access the same sample history without data duplication.

\subsection{Frame Synchronization Module}
The sync detector operates at the 4$\times$ oversampling rate. It reads samples from the shared RAM and computes the differential correlation with a pre-stored sync template. An adaptive thresholding calculating module runs in parallel. When the correlation output exceeds the adaptive threshold, a ``Frame Sync'' signal is asserted, triggering the FSM to transition to the frequency estimation state.

\subsection{Frequency and Timing Estimation}
Upon sync detection, the frequency estimator retrieves the preamble samples. It implements the L\&R algorithm using a coordinate rotation digital computer (CORDIC) core to extract the phase angle. The estimated CFO value is then used to generate a compensation phasor.
Timing recovery is performed by analyzing the phase peaks of the preamble's ``10'' pattern. The module calculates the average timing error $\bar{\epsilon}$ over multiple symbol periods and adjusts the read pointer of the shared RAM to the optimal sampling instant for the payload data.

\subsection{Demodulation and Resource Utilization}
The demodulator reads the corrected and time-aligned samples from the RAM. It performs multi-sample differential detection by averaging $N_d=7$ pairs of differential products per symbol. The decision bits are passed to a parallel CRC-16 checker.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a burst GFSK noncoherent receiver with differential correlation sync, L\&R CFO estimation, phase-peak timing recovery, and multi-sample differential demodulation. MATLAB and FPGA results show the design meets 500 kHz@10 MHz with target BER at 14/17 dB. Future work: further resource/power reduction, higher rates, and ASIC exploration.
